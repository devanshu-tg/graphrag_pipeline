Two scoring changes, applied to all three arms — and every arm gained
Two scoring changes, applied to all three arms. First, the rubric was corrected: it had been penalising answers for omitting derived detail — gold written as "$57,048M (~$57.0B), down 2% YoY" against an answer of "$57,048 million", which is that fact exactly. The rubric now treats the same number in another unit as identical, a ticker as a company name, and citations as ignorable. It still does not forgive a missing fact. Every arm gained and the weakest gained most — LLM-only +3.6, Basic RAG +2.1, GraphRAG +0.9 — so the gap over the baseline narrowed, which is the evidence the change was not bent in our favour. A third, more lenient rubric was built and rejected: it scored 100 to answers reciting real figures from the wrong fiscal year.
Two gold answers were wrong, and are corrected here
Two gold answers were wrong and are corrected here. EQ25 and EQ37 omitted Citigroup from the KPMG-audited list. The corpus is explicit — "/s/ KPMG LLP — auditor since 1969", across 20 Citigroup chunks. All three arms had listed it and all three were marked down for being more complete than the key. Correcting it gained GraphRAG 2 and the baseline 1. The frozen answer key on disk is not edited; the correction lives in build/make_sec_published.py.
Contamination is visible here and absent on the enterprise set
Contamination is visible here and absent on the enterprise set. With no retrieval at all, LLM-only scores 57% on real filings and 0.8% on the fictional corpus. The model has read these filings. That is why the enterprise result is the one to trust — and why the leak gate matters.
The judge inflates every arm here
The judge inflates every arm here. It awarded 75% to Walmart $611,289M; Apple $383,285M when gold was $713.2B and $416.2B — real figures from the wrong fiscal year. For financial QA a wrong number is a wrong answer, so strict pass is the figure to trust, not the percentage.
The graph wins the hardest tier and loses the easiest
The graph wins the hardest tier and loses the easiest. On aggregation it is 13/20 against 4/20 — 3x the baseline. On single lookup it is 7/8 against 8/8: for one number in one filing, vector retrieval is simply the better tool and the planner call is wasted overhead. A system that wins everything is a system that has not been tested properly.

How it works

Two builds, one comparison harness.

The diagram below is the SEC round: filings chunked, entities and relations extracted by an LLM in two passes, canonicalised into a curated graph, then three pipelines judged blind. The enterprise build reuses the harness and replaces the expensive half — see the note under the diagram.

What the enterprise build does differently. Four things in that diagram do not apply to it. No extraction: every document already names its author, its channel and its project, so 128,282 provenance edges came from metadata fields at zero LLM cost and 100% coverage — there is no two-phase Gemini pass and no canonicalisation step. No ChromaDB: TigerGraph Savanna is the vector store for both retrieval arms, which is what removes the vector store as a variable. The graph is enterprise, not finance, and the judge is gemini-3.1-pro-preview rather than gpt-4o-mini.
The baseline retrieves through TigerGraph too. Both arms use the same vector store, the same embedder and the same corpus, so the only difference between them is whether the graph is consulted. And the baseline is the reranked one — a cross-encoder over a 200-candidate pool, which took it from 0.30 to 0.53 set-F1 at the same token cost. Beating a weak baseline would prove nothing.

Read strict pass, not the judge
Read strict pass, not the judge. Strict means the answer set matches gold exactly — no partial credit. The judge is a lenient second opinion and it flatters the baseline: Basic RAG scores 62.3% judged but only 26/50 exactly right. Where the two disagree, strict is the number of record.
The budgets are deliberately unequal, in the baseline’s favour
The budgets are deliberately unequal, in the baseline's favour. GraphRAG reads 3 chunks; Basic RAG reads 10. Ten is where the reranked baseline scores best — cutting it to 3 drops its retrieval hit@k from 82.5% to 75.0% — so it is shown at its strongest while GraphRAG reads less. Not measured: the baseline end-to-end at a 3-chunk budget.
A fixed share of GraphRAG’s tokens is the planner call
1,185 of GraphRAG's 2,733 tokens are the planner call — 43% of its spend, and a fixed cost that does not shrink with the chunk budget. That is the floor on the token claim, not a number to leave out.
BERTScore is shown and is not the number of record
BERTScore is shown and is not the number of record. Here it happens to rank the arms correctly (0.852 / 0.934 / 1.000). On the SEC set it put LLM-only above Basic RAG (0.839 vs 0.830) while LLM-only got 5 of 50 right — it rewards fluent wrong answers. A metric that only works when you already know the answer is not evidence.
This is the whole result — the only variable is whether the question needs a join
This is the whole result. Basic RAG gets 0 of 20 exactly right on multi-hop and 10 of 10 on directory lookups — same corpus, same model, same questions. The only variable is whether the question needs a join. Five of those 20 questions require combining 102–1,065 documents against a 10-chunk budget: no reranker fixes that, because nothing puts 1,065 documents into 10 slots.
